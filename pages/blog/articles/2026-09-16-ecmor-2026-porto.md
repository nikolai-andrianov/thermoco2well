The THERMOCO2WELL project is approaching its end, so it's the right time to present the results to a wider audience — and the [ECMOR](https://eage.eventsair.com/ecmor-2026/) conference in Porto was a perfect venue for just that!

My presentation covered the wellbore flow modeling part, which addresses the following key challenges:

- Energy conservation — tracking how heat moves along the well as cold CO₂ flows down into a warm reservoir
- Phase transitions — CO₂ can flip between liquid and vapor inside the well
- Heat exchange with the surrounding rock — the well constantly exchanges heat to the formation and casing around it
- Friction and pressure drop — the two-phase mixture of CO₂ and brine creates a pressure loss that depends on how "rough" or turbulent the flow is

### Why phase transitions are tricky

When CO₂ is injected cold (e.g. 8°C) into a well that's initially full of warm brine, something interesting happens at start-up: the CO₂ arriving at the wellhead is briefly a two-phase mixture of liquid and vapor, sitting right on the boundary between the two states (the "saturation line"). Most simulators track temperature as the main variable, but temperature behaves badly right at that boundary — small changes in temperature don't tell you whether you're gaining or losing mass to the phase change, which makes the numerical solver struggle to converge.

Our trick: instead of tracking temperature, we track enthalpy (basically, heat content) together with pressure. Unlike temperature, enthalpy changes smoothly across the phase transition, so the solver sees a well-behaved problem instead of a numerical cliff edge. Temperature is then recovered afterwards from a quick lookup, rather than solved for directly.

### Putting it to the test: Stenlille

We applied the model to a real case: injecting CO₂ into the Gassum sandstone formation at Stenlille, onshore Denmark — the same formation that's stored natural gas safely since 1989. Injecting 10 tonnes/hour of CO₂ at 8°C, here's what we found over a 24-hour simulation:

- The brine-filled well unloads fast — within about 13 minutes, the wellhead pressure locks onto the CO₂ saturation pressure (~43.5 bar) while a two-phase mixture sits at the top of the well.
- Once the well is fully filled with liquid CO₂, wellhead pressure climbs, peaking at 72.8 bar after about 5 hours before settling to a quasi-steady 69.5 bar.
- Down at the reservoir, the CO₂ arrives warmed by the surrounding rock, but still about 22°C colder than the original formation temperature — and that temperature contrast is exactly the number a geomechanical engineer needs to assess whether thermal stresses could crack the cement or casing.

### What's next

The wellbore model is now fully coupled to a two-phase, non-isothermal reservoir simulator, both built as open-source code in MRST — so the whole thing is freely available for anyone to use or extend. Next up: more injection scenarios (higher rates, shut-in/restart cycles), and linking the thermal results to a geomechanical model to directly assess fracturing risk.
